Understanding the Waveform

The waveform shows how air pressure varies over time. The horizontal axis is time (milliseconds), and the vertical axis is amplitude (from -1 to +1, representing the digital signal range).

x(t) = A · sin(2πf t + φ)
AAmplitude — how loud the sound is
fFrequency — how fast it oscillates (pitch)
φPhase — starting position of the wave
tTime in seconds

05 Live Analysis Readouts
BEAT
♫ Detected Pitch
—
Autocorrelation
r[τ] = (1/N) · Σ x[n] · x[n+τ] / r[0]
f₀ = fs / τpeak

Pitch is detected via normalized autocorrelation. The first peak after zero-lag gives the fundamental period. The confidence bar shows detection reliability.

▶ BPM
—
Energy Flux Beat Detection
E(t) = (1/K) · Σ |X[k]|²
beat ↔ E(t) > C · Ēavg

Beats are detected when instantaneous spectral energy exceeds the rolling average by a factor of 1.4. BPM is derived from the median of recent inter-beat intervals.

▲ RMS (dBFS)
—
RMS & dBFS
RMS = √((1/N) · Σ x[n]²)
dBFS = 20 · log10(RMS)

dBFS measures digital amplitude relative to full scale (0 = maximum). It is NOT calibrated to sound pressure level (dB SPL). A 6 dB increase roughly doubles amplitude.

△ Peak Freq
—
Peak Frequency
fpeak = kpeak · fs / N

The peak frequency is the FFT bin with the highest magnitude. It indicates the dominant frequency component. The value is quantized to bin spacing (f_s/N).

ψ Centroid
—
Spectral Centroid
SC = Σ fk · |X[k]| / Σ |X[k]|

The spectral centroid is the weighted average frequency of the spectrum. It measures timbral "brightness" — high values indicate treble-heavy sound, low values indicate bass-heavy sound.

06 Experiments
Experiment Comparison
01 FFT Size — Time-Frequency Trade-off
Switch between FFT 512 and 8192 while speaking or humming. At 512, the waveform updates rapidly but frequency bars are coarse (86 Hz spacing at 44.1 kHz). At 8192, harmonics become visible but the display feels sluggish (5.4 Hz updates). Use the Capture A/B buttons to compare. This demonstrates the uncertainty principle: Δt · Δf ≥ 1/(4π).
Steps: 1. Click Start Experiment to configure. 2. Hum a steady note. 3. Capture A at FFT 8192. 4. Switch to 512. 5. Capture B. 6. Compare.
02 Signal Generator — Fourier Series Visualization
Switch source to Signal Generator. Set a 440 Hz sine wave — the spectrum shows one peak. Switch to square — odd harmonics appear (1320, 2200, 3080 Hz). Switch to sawtooth — all harmonics (880, 1320, 1760 Hz). This visually demonstrates the Fourier series decomposition of periodic waveforms.
Steps: 1. Click Start Experiment (sets sine 440 Hz). 2. Observe single peak. 3. Switch to square → odd harmonics appear. 4. Switch to sawtooth → all harmonics. 5. Compare with Capture A/B.
03 Window Function — Spectral Leakage Comparison
Generate a sine wave at a frequency that doesn't fall on a bin boundary. Enable the rectangular window overlay — you'll see energy spread into neighboring bins (leakage). Switch to Hann or Hamming overlay — the peak is cleaner but slightly broader. Compare bin spacing vs. actual resolution in the readout panel.
Steps: 1. Click Start Experiment (sets sine 440 Hz, FFT 4096). 2. Set rectangular window overlay → observe leakage. 3. Capture A. 4. Switch to Hann overlay. 5. Capture B. 6. Compare peak width.
04 Harmonic Analyzer — Harmonic Series Detection
Generate a sawtooth wave at 220 Hz and enable the harmonic toggle. The analyzer labels harmonic peaks at 220, 440, 660, 880 Hz (integer multiples). Switch to square — only odd harmonics are confirmed. The reliability indicator shows confidence based on harmonic alignment.
Steps: 1. Click Start Experiment (sawtooth 220 Hz + harmonics on). 2. Observe labeled peaks. 3. Capture A. 4. Switch to square. 5. Only odd harmonics remain. 6. Capture B → compare.
05 dBFS — Digital Amplitude vs Perceived Loudness
Using the microphone, speak at different volumes. The RMS readout shows dBFS — digital amplitude relative to full scale, not calibrated SPL. A 6 dB increase roughly doubles amplitude but does not double perceived loudness (that requires ~10 dB). Adjust gain to see clipping at 0 dBFS.
Steps: 1. Click Start Experiment (mic source). 2. Speak softly → note RMS (~-30 dBFS). 3. Capture A. 4. Speak loudly → note RMS (~-15 dBFS). 5. Capture B. 6. Compare the ~15 dB difference.
07 Learn

Sound is a mechanical wave — pressure variations propagating through air at approximately 343 m/s (at 20°C). The human ear perceives pressure oscillations in the range of 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz.

Wave equation
∂²p/∂t² = c² · ∇²p

where p is acoustic pressure and c is the speed of sound.

Digital sampling
fNyquist = fs / 2

At 44.1 kHz sample rate, frequencies up to 22.05 kHz can be represented.

The frequency domain decomposes a signal into its constituent frequencies. The FFT spectrum shows magnitude (vertical) against frequency (horizontal, log scale). Each bar represents the energy at a particular frequency band.

Click on peaks in the spectrum chart above to inspect individual frequencies. The spectrogram shows how the frequency content changes over time.

→ Go to Spectrum

The Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) decomposes a time-domain signal into its frequency components:

DFT Definition
X[k] = Σn=0N-1 x[n] · e-2πi·k·n/N

The FFT computes this in O(N log N). Bin k corresponds to frequency:

fk = k · fs / N

Important: bin spacing (fs/N) is the distance between adjacent FFT output bins. It is not the same as frequency resolution — the ability to distinguish two simultaneous tones depends on the window's main-lobe width (see Window Functions).

→ Go to Spectrum

Digital audio samples a continuous sound wave at discrete time intervals. The sample rate fs determines the highest frequency that can be represented:

fNyquist = fs / 2

At 44.1 kHz (CD quality), frequencies up to 22.05 kHz can be accurately represented. Frequencies above the Nyquist limit cause aliasing — they appear as false lower frequencies in the spectrum. An anti-aliasing filter removes frequencies above fs/2 before sampling.

→ Go to Waveform

Window functions taper the signal at the boundaries to reduce spectral leakage. There is a trade-off: lower sidelobes (less leakage) come at the cost of a wider main lobe (poorer frequency resolution). The AnalyserNode uses a Blackman window by default; the overlay feature lets you compare rectangular, Hann, and Hamming windows.

Rectangular
w[n] = 1

Main lobe: 2 bins · Sidelobes: -13 dB

Hann
w[n] = 0.5(1 - cos(2πn/(N-1)))

Main lobe: 4 bins · Sidelobes: -31 dB

→ Go to Spectrum

Harmonic analysis searches for peaks at integer multiples of the fundamental (fh = h·f0). Only peaks that align with the harmonic series and exceed 5% of the fundamental amplitude are labeled.

fh = h · f0, h = 1, 2, 3, …

A sawtooth wave contains all harmonics with amplitudes decreasing as 1/n. A square wave contains only odd harmonics. A triangle wave contains odd harmonics with 1/n² amplitudes. The pattern of harmonics determines timbre — what makes a violin sound different from a flute.

→ Go to Spectrum

Pitch detection uses normalized autocorrelation:

r[τ] = (1/N) · Σ x[n] · x[n+τ] / r[0]

The first peak after zero-lag gives the fundamental period. Frequency = fs / τ.
